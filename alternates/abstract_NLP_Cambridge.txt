Prompt sensitivity in elicited model judgments is well documented, and the unreliability of
LLM-as-a-judge scoring is by now a standard caveat. What is missing is the size of these effects
measured together, on one corpus, against the decisions an evaluation designer actually makes.
We report three such magnitudes over 7,920 elicitations across six frontier model families, and
then measure them again on a second, unrelated construct family.

Threshold placement first, because it is the largest and the least discussed. Models answer on a
coarse numeric grid — 46 distinct values across 7,852 elicited components, 89.5% of them
multiples of 0.1 — so a decision rule compared against a fixed constant adjudicates a large
share of cases by rounding. In our worked case the cut sits exactly on the modal value: moving
from a strict to a non-strict comparison moves a control condition from 0.000 to 0.778, and on
the second family moving one cut from 0.60 to 0.90 changes a reported rate by a factor of 16
without touching the data.

Instruction framing second, where the natural ablation gives the wrong answer. Deleting the
sentence that names the measured behaviour barely moves the result; deleting the surrounding
framing removes it. Running only the first half exonerates the prompt. On the second family the
framing effect does not replicate at all, and the reason generalises: framing is worth what the
model could not have inferred without it.

Item sampling third. Between-item standard deviations equal or exceed the mean, so a single-item
probe carries a 95% interval half-width of +/-0.41; reaching +/-0.05 takes 69 items in one
family and 20 in the other.

No magnitude transfers between the two families. We give the design rule each result implies,
and release both banks, the code and every generation.
