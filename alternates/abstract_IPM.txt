Systems that evaluate language models increasingly do so by asking a model for a number about a
statement — a confidence, a relevance score, a rating, a degree of some property — and then
treating that number as a measurement. The design is now embedded in retrieval evaluation, in
LLM-as-a-judge pipelines, in self-critique loops and in most bespoke probes built for a single
study. We quantify three ways in which such a pipeline produces the score as much as the model
does, on 7,920 elicitations across six frontier model families and two unrelated construct
families.

The first is item sampling. A rate computed on one statement is a property of that statement:
between-item standard deviations are of the same order as the mean and exceed it in four of the
five constructs, so a single-item probe carries a 95%
interval half-width of +/-0.41 on a [0,1] rate. A published figure of 0.661 obtained from one
sentence becomes 0.222 over ten. Sizing a test bank for a +/-0.05 half-width takes 69 items in
one family and 20 in the other, and nothing short of measuring tells you which regime you are in.

The second is instruction framing, where the obvious ablation misleads. Deleting the sentence
that names the measured behaviour barely moves the result; deleting the surrounding framing
removes it entirely. A single-step ablation would have exonerated the instrument.

The third is threshold placement. Models answer on a coarse numeric grid, so a cut placed on a
modal value adjudicates a large share of cases by rounding: moving one cut from 0.60 to 0.90
changes a reported rate by a factor of 16 without touching the data.

Replicating all three on an unrelated task shows that no magnitude transfers intact and that the
framing effect does not replicate in the mean, though it still moves the shape of the response,
which sharpens the rule rather than weakening it. We
state the design rule each result implies, and release both item banks, the analysis code and
every raw generation.
